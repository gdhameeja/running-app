# Presenting a weekly personal performance report, and measuring bodyweight strength and walking progress

Source-check key used below. **[abstract]** means the abstract or full text was read through Europe PMC or a repository copy. **[full text]** means the relevant passage was read directly. **[digest]** means the claim came from a search-result digest or secondary coverage and the original was not opened, so check it before quoting it as fact.

## What makes self-tracking feedback actionable rather than ignored or anxiety-inducing?

### Takeaway
Monitoring works when it is recorded, tied to a goal and aimed at the task. Feedback often backfires when it pulls attention to the self. False negative scores can worsen how people feel and perform, even when nothing has changed physically, so the report should describe behaviour and avoid verdicts on the person.

### Cited Findings

**Feedback theory**
- Feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = .41, 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations), but over one third of them lowered performance. Sampling error, feedback sign and existing theories could not explain this. [abstract] — [Kluger and DeNisi 1996, Hebrew University record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)
- Feedback intervention theory says effectiveness falls as attention moves up from task learning, through task motivation, to meta-task or self-related processes. [abstract] — [Kluger and DeNisi 1996](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)

**Self-monitoring and behaviour change techniques**
- Across 122 evaluations (N = 44,747) of healthy eating and physical activity interventions, the pooled effect was 0.31. Self-monitoring explained the most heterogeneity (13%). Interventions combining self-monitoring with at least one other control-theory technique (such as goals or feedback) did better, 0.42 versus 0.26. [digest of abstract] — [Michie et al. 2009](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/13754)
- In 138 randomised studies (N = 19,951), prompting progress monitoring promoted goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.48). Physical activity studies showed d+ = 0.59. [full text] — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/)
- Physically recording monitoring data beat not recording it (d+ = 0.43 vs 0.29; 0.57 vs 0.23 when attainment was measured objectively). Monitoring that was public or reported to someone beat private monitoring (0.55 and 0.47 vs 0.19). [full text] — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/)
- Comparing the current state with a past reference value worked as well as comparing it with a future target (d+ = 0.43 vs 0.41, p = .71). Monitoring rate of progress versus distance from goal made no difference, and neither did passive versus active monitoring. [full text] — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/)

**Wearables: mixed evidence**
- An umbrella review of 39 systematic reviews (163,992 participants) found trackers improved physical activity (SMD 0.3 to 0.6), equal to about 1,800 extra steps per day and 40 minutes more walking per day. Psychosocial outcomes such as quality of life were typically small and often non-significant. [abstract] — [Ferguson et al. 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35868813)
- In the TRIPPA trial (n = 800), a tracker alone did not significantly raise activity at 6 months (16 min/week, 95% CI -2 to 35), while cash and charity incentives did. No health outcome improved in any arm. This partly contradicts the umbrella review. [abstract] — [Finkelstein et al. 2016](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27717766)
- Patel, Asch and Volpp frame wearables as "facilitators, not drivers" of behaviour change. Only the title and record were available, so the argument itself is not checked here. — [Patel et al. 2015](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25569175)

**Harm: orthosomnia and nocebo effects**
- Baron and colleagues described patients seeking treatment for self-diagnosed sleep problems based on tracker data. The inferred link between tracker data and daytime fatigue "may become a perfectionistic quest for the ideal sleep". Patients often found tracker data more consistent with their experience than validated tests. This is case-level evidence and not a prevalence estimate. [abstract] — [Baron et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27855740)
- Orthosomnia is not a formally recognised sleep disorder. [digest] — [Sleep.com](https://sleep.com/sleep-tech/orthosomnia)
- 63 people with insomnia were randomised to sham positive or negative sleep-efficiency feedback. The negative group reported worse evening alertness (d = 0.79) and more sleepiness or fatigue (d = 0.55). Psychomotor vigilance and sleep-related attentional bias did not differ, so feedback shifted appraisal rather than objective performance. [digest of abstract] — [Gavriloff et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29989248/), [Oxford record](https://www.ndcn.ox.ac.uk/publication/854408)
- In two experiments (164 participants in total), students falsely told their sleep quality was poor, based on a sham REM measure, performed worse on cognitive tests, while self-reported sleep did not predict performance. Telling them it was good improved performance. This conflicts in part with Gavriloff, where objective vigilance did not change. [digest] — [Draganich and Erdal 2014 PDF copy](https://www.noigroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Draganich_2014_sleep-fatigue-beliefs.pdf), [Smithsonian summary](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/you-can-get-placebo-sleep-180949410)

**Motivation**
- A review of 66 studies found consistent support for a positive relation between autonomous motivation and exercise. Identified regulation predicted adoption more strongly, intrinsic motivation predicted long-term adherence, and competence satisfaction predicted participation. Most of the studies were non-experimental. [digest of abstract] — [Teixeira et al. 2012](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1479-5868-9-78)
- In one set of studies, counting steps increased walking but lowered enjoyment, including among people who chose to be measured. The proposed mechanism is that measurement makes an activity feel like work. In a reading study, framing the tracking around what could be learned removed the effect. [digest] — [Etkin 2016](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Etkin-Jordan-11-12-15-Hidden-Cost.pdf), [Fuqua summary](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps)
- A low-tier 2021 survey of avid cyclists reported the opposite, with quantification adding enjoyment for most. [digest] — [IJPBA 2021](https://www.graphyonline.com/archives/IJPBA/2021/IJPBA-174/)
- Among 159 former tracker users, permanent abandonment was linked especially to loss of tracking motivation. [digest] — [Attig and Franke 2020](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/abandonment-of-personal-quantification-a-review-and-empirical-stu/)
- Former trackers reported that tools made them feel judged ("I felt guilty every time I tracked an expense"). Guilt about stopping was more common among people who tracked to change a habit (16.2% of activity trackers). [full text] — [Epstein et al. 2016](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28503678)

### Inferences
- Harkin's result on past versus future reference supports comparing with one's own past as the default. No external score is needed for feedback to work.
- The report should pair every number with a goal or next step, because self-monitoring alone is the weaker form in Michie's analysis.
- Keeping a visible archive of past reports approximates "physically recorded". A private app cannot provide the "reported to someone" effect by itself. An optional export or share to a friend or coach would be the nearest substitute, and that is untested in this setting.
- Avoid composite verdicts such as "readiness 54" or "poor week". The nocebo evidence comes from sleep feedback, and extending it to training or readiness scores is an extrapolation, but the design cost of avoiding them is low.
- If the app derives a score from noisy data such as GPS or estimated effort, show uncertainty. Feedback that is wrong and negative is the worst case in the sham studies.
- Use a learning frame ("what this week showed") and avoid a scoring frame, following Etkin's reading result.

### Gaps
- I found no study testing a nocebo effect from negative training readiness or recovery scores specifically. The evidence is from sleep feedback, in people with insomnia and in students.
- The number of cases in Baron 2017 was not verified, because the full text could not be retrieved.
- Hattie and Timperley's feedback model and a dedicated goal-setting meta-analysis for physical activity were not retrieved.

## How many metrics, what language, how to frame misses, and how to handle low-data weeks and streaks?

### Takeaway
No trial sets an ideal metric count, so "few metrics plus one recommendation" is a design judgement consistent with the theory and not an evidence-based number. The evidence on broken streaks is direct: displaying a break reduces later engagement, especially when people blame themselves, and repair options soften this.

### Cited Findings
- Streaks are behaviours performed three or more times consecutively. Across seven studies, showing an intact streak raised later engagement compared with showing a broken one, regardless of actual past behaviour. Only how the log looked mattered. [digest of abstract] — [Silverman and Barasch 2023](https://udspace.udel.edu/handle/19716/34160)
- The effect of a broken streak was stronger when people blamed themselves and weaker when they could repair the streak. [digest] — [Silverman and Barasch 2023, University of Colorado summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)
- The authors' press coverage suggests apps may do better not to emphasise broken streaks in notifications, a suggestion flagged as untested. [digest] — [University of Delaware](https://lerner.udel.edu/seeing-opportunity/lerner-professor-researches-how-streaks-motivate-us/)
- Gym visits, goal commitments and "diet" searches rise after temporal landmarks such as a new week, month or birthday. The findings concern initiation and do not show lasting attainment, and one later randomised trial of medication reminders found no significant advantage from fresh-start timing. [digest] — [Dai, Milkman and Riis 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:60:y:2014:i:10:p:2563-2582)
- Gamification results are mixed. Points and levels showed no added weight loss over tracking with a partner in LOSE IT. GAMEPAD found a step increase in patients with peripheral artery disease (920 steps, P = .06 during the intervention; 1,074 steps, P = .03 at follow-up). [abstract] — [Kurtzman et al. 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30003481), [Fanaroff et al. 2025](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41334739)
- Commercial precedent for low-data handling: WHOOP's weekly assessment requires at least five days of logged data, and Oura reports require two weeks of total wear. Oura's Rest Mode reportedly pauses activity goals, scores and contributors. [Oura help page read; WHOOP and Rest Mode digest] — [WHOOP](https://whoop.com/thelocker/new-weekly-performance-assessment), [Oura Reports](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/360046061373-Oura-Reports), [Tech Advisor on Oura](https://www.techadvisor.com/article/2575433/)
- Oura labels readiness under 70 "pay attention" and marks contributors with a red progress bar. [digest] — [Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/readiness-score/)

### Inferences
Suggested rules for the app. These are judgement calls derived from the above.
- **Metrics:** 3 to 5 headline numbers, each with last week and a 4-week average. Details stay behind a tap.
- **One recommendation:** a single specific behaviour for next week, stated as a choice ("Try two strength days, for example Tuesday and Friday"). This fits Michie's goal-plus-feedback pairing and self-determination theory's emphasis on autonomy.
- **Plain language:** describe behaviour and comparison, not character. Prefer "2 runs this week, usually 3" to "You missed your target". Avoid red, "failed", "poor", "should" and "only".
- **Misses:** state them once, neutrally, attribute them to circumstances where the data allow it, and move straight to next week. Silverman and Barasch's self-blame finding supports external or neutral attribution.
- **Low-data weeks:** below a threshold (for example under 2 logged days), replace the comparison with a short "light week" note, skip trends and carry the last valid baseline. Exclude such weeks from averages or mark them clearly.
- **Declared breaks:** offer "illness, travel or rest" labels that pause comparisons, as Oura's Rest Mode does. Labelled weeks should not count against consistency.
- **Streaks:** use forgiving measures such as "active weeks in the last 8" or "3 of the last 4 weeks", which cannot collapse to zero after one miss. Avoid daily streaks, because a rest day is part of training. If a streak is shown, offer repair and never headline the break.
- **Tone on good weeks:** avoid inflation. Competence support needs to be credible, so praise should point to something specific that happened.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled evidence on the ideal number of metrics in a personal health digest.
- I found no controlled test of plain-language versus technical wording in fitness reports.
- The streak studies use consumer behaviour logs, and how well they transfer to training is untested. I found no direct trial of streak effects on overtraining or on training through injury, so that risk is plausible but unsupported.
- The Rest Mode description appeared in a search digest, and Oura's own page could not be opened.

## Weekly versus daily cadence, digest design and good examples

### Takeaway
Direct evidence comparing weekly with daily feedback is missing. The case for weekly is that it suits slow-moving outcomes, makes noise less prominent and has commercial precedent. Reserve in-the-moment prompts for moments when there is something to do.

### Cited Findings
- WHOOP's Weekly Performance Assessment shows strain and recovery balance plus sleep and activity trends, is delivered on Mondays, and checks sleep timing consistency. [digest; page returned 403] — [WHOOP](https://whoop.com/thelocker/new-weekly-performance-assessment)
- WHOOP's monthly report has reportedly been replaced by a "Month in Review", and at least one user complained it was a step backward from the detailed version. This is a forum source. [digest] — [WHOOP community](https://www.community.whoop.com/t/new-month-in-review-is-a-huge-disappointment/9035)
- Oura's weekly report is a short recap showing average Readiness, Sleep and Activity scores with trend charts for contributors such as resting heart rate, HRV, sleep time and goal completion. It appears on the Today tab and dismissed reports stay accessible in a Reports menu. [full text] — [Oura Reports](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/360046061373-Oura-Reports)
- Monarch's Weekly Recap is a short weekly summary of major changes across spending, savings, income and net worth. It can be removed from the dashboard or switched off. [digest] — [Monarch help](https://help.monarch.com/hc/en-us/articles/16116906962452-About-Monarch-s-AI-Features)
- A third-party review describes it as a dashboard card with follow-up questions. [digest] — [Personal Finance Tools review](https://personal-finance-tools.pages.dev/blog/monarch-money-winter-2026-review)
- Commentators attribute Spotify Wrapped's appeal to flattering self-description, story form, a consistent template and annual scarcity. These are opinion pieces and marketing analysis, not evaluations of behaviour change. [digest] — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/12/03/why-spotify-wrapped-marketing-genius-individuality-uniqueness-belonging-psychology/), [Design Breadcrumbs](https://designbreadcrumbs.beehiiv.com/p/wrapped-by-design)
- In HeartSteps, a contextually tailored suggestion versus none raised the following 30-minute step count by 14% on average (35 steps, p = .06). The effect was uneven over time, with a larger initial effect (66%, 167 steps). This fits habituation, though the abstract section retrieved does not spell out the decline. [abstract] — [Klasnja et al. 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30192907)
- Trials acknowledge notification frequency as an open question. A 2020 mHealth trial called for research on message timing and frequency. [digest] — [JMIR mHealth 2020](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2020/2/e13747/PDF)

### Inferences
- Deliver at the start of the week, on Sunday evening or Monday morning. The new week is a temporal landmark, and this matches WHOOP's Monday delivery. The recommendation then applies to a week that has not started yet.
- Use one notification per week at most, and make it informative in itself ("Your week: 3 runs, 2 walks. One idea for next week inside."). No reminders if it goes unopened. A personal app does not need to chase engagement.
- Keep a fixed layout, as with Wrapped's template, so that the eye learns where to look. A suggested order:
  1. A one-line summary of the week.
  2. One thing that went well.
  3. 3 to 5 numbers versus own recent past.
  4. One observation.
  5. One recommendation.
  6. An optional reflection prompt.
- Keep an archive, as Oura does. It provides Harkin's "recorded" benefit and allows month-over-month review.
- Borrow Wrapped's story and specificity for occasional milestones such as a 12-week look-back. Do not copy its annual scarcity or social orientation into a weekly report.
- Do not use Oura's red "pay attention" pattern, for the reasons in the first section. That is a judgement and I found no study of this specific design.
- Slow metrics such as push-up max or a fitness trend should be reviewed monthly, because a week is too short to show a real change.

### Gaps
- I found no trial directly randomising weekly versus daily feedback for physical activity.
- Monarch's email delivery is unclear. Reviews and forum snapshots disagree about weekly versus monthly emails.
- I could not confirm the current state of WHOOP's weekly report, because its page returned 403 and the sources are older.

## What do running coaches review in a weekly check-in?

### Takeaway
Coaching sources agree on the same core sequence: ask how it felt first, compare planned with completed, look at the data, and agree what comes next. The evidence is mainly professional practice, with some support for pairing perceived effort with duration.

### Cited Findings
- Ultrarunning coach Jason Koop says "How did you feel?" is the first question he asks after a workout. He argues subjective feedback is more useful than watch metrics, while still valuing technical data over time. Repeated words such as "good" or "sluggish" across similar sessions reveal strengths and weaknesses. [full text] — [Koop, TrainRight](https://trainright.com/power-of-subjective-feedback-athlete-training/)
- A weekly check-in of about five questions, kept the same each week so answers can be compared, is one coaching-platform recommendation. It includes completed versus planned sessions. [digest; vendor source] — [QuickCoach](https://www.quickcoach.fit/client-check-in-questions.html)
- Another vendor recommends covering compliance, recovery, nutrition and mindset, with 1 to 10 scales and one or two open questions. [digest; vendor source] — [QuickCoach](https://www.quickcoach.fit/online-coaching-client-check-ins.html)
- A training journal asks athletes to summarise their week: "What went well? Where did you struggle?" The coach then replies. This comes from a wrestling journal, not a running source. [digest] — [All In Wrestlers Journal](https://intercom.help/all-in-wrestlers-journal/en/articles/10310264-setting-up-weekly-performance-evaluations)
- An Australian sport guide recommends opening a post-session review with something positive. [digest; original PDF no longer reachable] — [Sport Australia](https://www.sportaus.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/684991/The_post-session_review.pdf)
- A TrainingPeaks coach pairs athlete reflection with data review and a call at the end of the season. [digest] — [TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/season-over-what-does-a-coach-do/)
- Combining session RPE with duration gave a more individualised estimate of week-to-week training stress in runners than duration alone. [digest] — [Napier et al. 2020](https://nata.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/attr/55/12/article-p1285.xml)
- Session RPE is usually collected about 30 minutes after a session and multiplied by duration. [digest] — [Barnes 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5645301)

### Inferences
A personal app can imitate a coach's review with this sequence:
1. **Athlete's voice first.** One tap: "How did the week feel?" (for example easy, about right, hard, too much). Show this answer next to the numbers.
2. **Planned versus done.** Use neutral counts by session type.
3. **What went well.** Choose it automatically from the data, such as longest run, best consistency or a strength first.
4. **What to notice.** One pattern only, such as "Easy runs were faster than usual and the week felt hard".
5. **One focus for next week.**

Suggested reflection prompts, at most one per week, rotating (my synthesis, not from a single source):
- "What was your best session and why?"
- "What got in the way, if anything?"
- "How did your legs feel on the last run?"
- "Any niggle worth watching?"
- "What is one thing you want to keep doing?"

Keep the fixed set stable so that answers can be compared across weeks. Pain or niggle answers should change the recommendation, and should override any push toward more volume.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study of weekly check-in formats. Most sources are coach blogs and vendor templates.
- "One focus" is common coaching advice, but I found no reputable running source stating it explicitly, and I did not find relevant material from Magness or Uphill Athlete.
- The 2016 Saw review on subjective monitoring was not retrieved.

## How should bodyweight strength progress be measured, and does strength training help runners?

### Takeaway
Track hard sets per movement pattern, session frequency, and the hardest variation performed for a target rep range. Total reps alone mislead, because moving to a harder variation lowers reps while strength goes up. Two sessions a week is the evidence-backed baseline. For running, the case is good for performance, but it rests mainly on heavy and plyometric work, and injury evidence for runners specifically is weaker than the headline numbers suggest.

### Cited Findings

**Load, effort and bodyweight validity**
- Muscle growth can be achieved across a wide range of loads, while maximal strength benefits most from heavy loads. [digest of abstract] — [Schoenfeld et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)
- In 18 untrained men, 8 weeks of twice-weekly push-ups, adjusted to match 40% of bench press 1RM and taken to failure, gave gains in 1RM and in triceps and pectoralis thickness comparable to bench press. Muscle endurance capacity and power did not change in either group. The sample is small. [digest of abstract] — [Kikuchi and Nakazato 2017](https://doaj.org/article/f70cce826f3348b5b4f3ace8cf8ac33e)
- In 23 moderately trained men, 4 weeks of progressive calisthenic push-ups (3 days a week), using harder variations to keep programming variables, improved upper-body strength. The authors report this as the first demonstration that progressive variations can improve upper-body strength. The comparison with the bench press group was cut off in the retrieved abstract, so it is not reported here. The duration is short and the sample small. [abstract, partly truncated] — [Kotarsky et al. 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29466268)
- Training to failure is not required for strength or size gains, and it is not harmful either (15 studies; strength ES -0.09; hypertrophy ES 0.22, both non-significant). Resistance-trained people showed a small hypertrophy benefit (ES 0.15). [abstract] — [Grgic et al. 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33497853)
- Set failure had a trivial advantage over non-failure for hypertrophy (ES 0.19), and momentary failure had no advantage over non-failure (ES 0.12). The authors suggest a non-linear relationship. [abstract] — [Refalo et al. 2023](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/36334240)
- Proximity to failure meaningfully influenced hypertrophy but not strength gain, in an exploratory meta-regression. [digest] — [Robinson, Pelland et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)
- People can estimate reps to failure within about 1 rep when close to failure (0 to 5 reps left on chest press), and error exceeds 2 reps when 7 to 10 reps remain. [abstract] — [Hackett et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27787474)
- Accuracy falls with more reps in a set and with lower relative load, across 26 studies. People tend to underpredict reps left by about one rep. Accuracy seems best at 12 reps or fewer. [digest; the 12-rep figure is from secondary summaries] — [Russo et al. 2025](https://dev.rdpc.dspace.uevora.pt/items/d6f78224-a6e5-4f67-b1b8-2c65c7e7f199), [MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/reps-in-reserve/)

**Quantifying variations**
- Peak ground reaction force as a fraction of body mass by push-up variation, from Ebben et al. 2011 Table 1 [full text]:

| Variation | Fraction of body mass |
|---|---|
| Feet on 61 cm box | 0.74 |
| Feet on 30 cm box | 0.70 |
| Regular | 0.64 |
| Hands on 30 cm box | 0.55 |
| Bent knee | 0.49 |
| Hands on 61 cm box | 0.41 |

  The authors suggest quantifying push-ups as a percentage of body mass. — [Ebben et al. 2011](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/4457/4146)
- Caution on that paper: Table 2 of the same paper, in newtons, does not match Table 1. Regular push-up is about 700 N, which is near 0.89 of the mean body weight of 80.6 kg, and the ranking differs for the lowest two conditions. Treat the values as a rough ladder and not as measured load. My calculation from the paper. — [Ebben et al. 2011](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/4457/4146)

**Volume and frequency**
- Each additional weekly set was associated with an effect size increase of 0.023, or 0.37% more gain. Higher versus lower volume differed by 3.9%. Across <5, 5 to 9 and 10+ sets per muscle per week there was only a trend (P = .074). [abstract] — [Schoenfeld et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27433992)
- A later meta-regression (*Sports Medicine* 2026, preprint 2024) counted indirect sets fractionally. It found returns diminish with volume, more steeply for strength than for hypertrophy. Only strength showed consistently identifiable effects of frequency. [digest of abstract] — [Pelland et al. record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en), [preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)
- Strength gains rose with frequency (ES 0.74, 0.82, 0.93, 1.08 for 1, 2, 3, 4+ times per week), but this appeared to be driven by volume, with no significant frequency effect once volume was equated. [abstract] — [Grgic et al. 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29470825)
- With volume equated, frequency does not meaningfully affect hypertrophy, so frequency can follow personal preference. [abstract] — [Schoenfeld et al. 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30558493)
- WHO recommends muscle-strengthening activity at moderate or greater intensity, involving all major muscle groups, on 2 or more days a week. It found no evidence supporting a dose-response with higher volumes for health outcomes. [full text] — [Bull et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/)
- Muscle-strengthening activity was associated with 10 to 17% lower risk of mortality and major diseases. The J-shaped curve showed the largest reduction at about 30 to 60 minutes per week. This is observational. [digest] — [Momma et al. 2022](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/56/13/755)

**Tests and norms**
- Among 1,104 male firefighters (mean age 39.6), those completing over 40 push-ups had much lower incidence of cardiovascular events over 10 years than those doing under 10 (IRR 0.04, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.36). There were only 37 events, the data are observational, and they may not generalise to women, other ages or less active men. [abstract] — [Yang et al. 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30768197), [press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/865316)
- Published push-up norms conflict for men aged 30 to 39. One band set gives 17 to 21 as "good", and another gives 34 to 41 as "good". One percentile reference gives a median of 19 and a 75th percentile of 22. These are all secondary sites. [digest] — [Sensai](https://www.sensai.fit/blog/push-up-test-norms-by-age-sex), [Topend Sports](https://www.topendsports.com/testing/tests/push-up.htm), [Fitness Norms](https://fitnessnorms.pages.dev/strength/push-ups/male/age-30-39)
- McGill reportedly sees no utility in very long plank holds and recommends repeated 10-second holds. [digest; press coverage] — [HuffPost](https://www.huffingtonpost.ca/2018/03/28/how-long-to-hold-a-plank_a_23397921/)
- A single 15-minute bout of isometric core exercises temporarily increased torso stiffness in 24 participants. [abstract] — [Lee and McGill 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28282756)

**Strength training for runners**
- In 24 studies of distance runners, strength training generally improved running economy by 2 to 8% against controls, though not in every study. Time trial (1.5 to 10 km) and sprint performance also tended to improve. VO2max and body composition were typically unaffected. The authors recommend adding 2 to 3 sessions a week of varied strength modalities. [abstract] — [Blagrove et al. 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29249083)
- The included modalities were heavy resistance, explosive resistance and plyometric training, so conventional high-rep bodyweight work was not the main form tested. [full text] — [Blagrove et al. 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29249083)
- A 2024 meta-analysis found high-load and plyometric training improved running economy, with plyometrics effective at slower speeds (at or below 12 km/h; small effect, ES -0.307) and high-load training better at higher speeds. No improvement was seen with submaximal or isometric strength training. Certainty was mostly moderate. [digest of abstract] — [Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01978-y)
- 8 plyometric jumping sessions over 4 weeks improved running economy by 2 to 7% in 19 orienteers, regardless of jumping surface. There was no non-jumping control group. [abstract] — [Lännerström et al. 2021](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34400998)
- Strength training cut sports injuries (RR 0.315, 95% CI 0.207 to 0.480) and overuse injuries were almost halved across all exercise types (RR 0.527). Stretching had no effect (RR 0.963). These were sports injuries generally. [abstract] — [Lauersen et al. 2014](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24100287)
- A later review of 6 trials (7,738 participants, 177 injuries) found RR 0.338 and reported that a 10% increase in strength training volume cut injury risk by over four percentage points. [abstract] — [Lauersen et al. 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30131332)
- Running-specific evidence contradicts the headline. In 720 first-time marathoners, a short, self-directed strength routine 3 times a week did not reduce overuse injury causing marathon non-completion (7.1% vs 7.3%, risk ratio 0.97). [digest of abstract] — [Toresdahl et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31642726/)

### Inferences
**What to measure, in order of priority**
1. **Strength days this week** (target 2 or more, per WHO). This is the simplest and best-supported number.
2. **Hard sets per movement pattern per week.** A hard set is one ending within about 3 reps of failure. The 3-rep cut-off is a common convention and was not derived from these papers. Suggested patterns:
   - Push: push-up family
   - Squat or single leg: split squats, step-ups
   - Hinge or posterior: single-leg bridges, hip thrusts
   - Pull
   - Core: anti-extension, side planks

   Use the Schoenfeld bands loosely: under 5 is a light dose, 5 to 9 is moderate, 10 or more is high. For a runner who is also doing leg-heavy running, the lower bands are likely enough for health and support. The 10+ band is only a statistical trend, so do not present it as a threshold.
3. **Progression level per pattern.** Keep an ordered ladder (for example wall, incline, knee, full, feet-elevated, archer or one-arm-assisted). Store the variation and reps. Double progression is the simple rule: when all sets reach the top of the range (say 12 reps), move up a variation and restart near the bottom (say 6 to 8 reps). Show this as "Level 4 of 7" or "full push-ups, 3 x 10".
4. **Best clean set per variation.** Use this as a personal record and compare only within the same variation.

**Avoiding false declines**
- When variation changes, reps fall. The report must show this as progress ("moved up to feet-elevated") and must not show a rep drop.
- Avoid raw total reps as the headline, because a week of easier variations would look better than a week of harder ones.
- An optional weighted figure of reps x load fraction is possible using Ebben's ladder, but the table inconsistency means it should remain an internal aid, not a displayed precise number. Most bodyweight exercises have no such data.

**Effort rating**
- Rep-in-reserve estimates are poor in long sets, so collect them only when a set is short, and prefer a simple tap such as "could have done 0, 1 to 2, 3 to 4, 5+ more".
- When sets exceed about 15 to 20 reps, the better response is to suggest a harder variation, since that also improves effort estimation.
- Failure is not necessary. The app should not push "to failure" as a goal, especially near hard runs.

**Testing**
- Run a monthly max-rep push-up test, using the same variation and the same form rule, and compare with one's own past results.
- Do not show the Yang thresholds as a target or risk score. A report saying "under 10 push-ups" alongside "96% lower risk" would be a nocebo-prone message from an observational firefighter sample.
- Do not show norm bands by default. They conflict, and sex and age need to be known.

**Planks**
- Treat planks as a form-based accessory, with several short quality holds instead of chasing a maximum time. Once a 60-second hold is comfortable, progress to harder variations such as side planks or bird dogs and do not lengthen the hold. The 60-second figure is my judgement and not a validated norm.

**No pull-up bar**
- Pulling is the obvious gap in the plan. Options include table or low-bar inverted rows if safe, towel or door-anchored rows, backpack rows and prone Y-T-W raises. No evidence was gathered on these, so this is a coaching-style suggestion. Show "pull" as a pattern, but do not nag if the person has no suitable equipment. A short note in settings can allow excluding it.

**For running**
- Be honest in the report: "supports your running" is fair, and "cuts injuries by two thirds" is not.
- If running economy is the aim, bodyweight plyometrics (hops, bounds, skipping, jump squats) are the no-equipment match to the evidence. Build them up gradually, and place them away from hard runs. Ground-contact counts (Blagrove's included trials used, for example, 60 to 180 foot contacts) are a usable metric.
- Isometric and submaximal work showed no running economy benefit in Llanos-Lagos, so plain bodyweight sets should be credited for general strength and health, not for running economy.
- Single-leg progressions raise relative load without equipment, which is the closest substitute for heavy work. That is a reasoned guess and I did not find a trial on it.

### Gaps
- Volume "landmarks" such as MEV, MAV and MRV are coaching constructs. I did not find peer-reviewed thresholds for them, and no volume data are specific to bodyweight training.
- Saeterbakken and other calisthenics reviews could not be verified. Search results for calisthenics were mostly low-quality, so there is no solid systematic review here. A 2023 trial of progressive bodyweight squats was reported only by an aggregator and was left out.
- Plank norms, and reliability data for plank hold time, were not found. Evidence linking plank time to running injury is thin and reportedly conflicting. The study of hold times in collegiate runners, and Luedke and Rauh's contrary result, could not be traced to primary sources.
- No primary CSEP or ACSM document was opened, so the push-up norm conflict is unresolved.
- Reliable load fractions exist only for push-ups. I found none for squats, lunges or rows.
- The Pelland figures for optimal sets were not extracted. Only abstract-level conclusions are reported.

## What does the evidence say about walking: steps, minutes, cadence and counting walks?

### Takeaway
Most of the benefit in step studies arrives by about 7,000 to 10,000 steps a day, and it starts well below 10,000. Walking at 100 steps per minute or faster is a fair proxy for moderate intensity, and WHO counts bouts of any length, so a walk of any duration can count, and brisk minutes can go toward the 150 to 300 weekly total.

### Cited Findings

**Guidelines**
- Adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity a week, or an equivalent combination. [full text] — [Bull et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/)
- Bouts of any duration now count, replacing the 2010 requirement of at least 10 minutes. Going beyond 300 moderate minutes is a conditional recommendation for additional benefit. [full text] — [Bull et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/)
- WHO states that "all physical activity counts" and "any amount of physical activity is better than none", and lists walking among popular ways to be active. [full text] — [WHO fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity)

**Steps**
- Across 15 international cohorts, more steps were associated with progressively lower all-cause mortality. Risk levelled off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day in adults aged 60 and older, and 8,000 to 10,000 in younger adults. Associations with stepping rate weakened after adjusting for step volume. [digest of abstract] — [Paluch et al. 2022](https://doaj.org/article/c3639c468b4348958202e52793ab979d)
- Compared with 2,000 steps a day, 7,000 was associated with 47% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.46 to 0.60). Inflection points fell around 5,000 to 7,000 for several outcomes. Benefits continued past 7,000 for some, such as heart disease. The analysis covered 57 studies with limited age-specific data and possible residual confounding. [digest of abstract] — [Ding et al. 2025](https://produccioncientifica.uca.es/documentos/68a0ccc0ea77332c3753748f), [News Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20250724/Walking-7000-steps-per-day-associated-with-reductions-in-risk-of-several-serious-health-problems.aspx)
- Among 4,840 US adults aged 40 and over, 8,000 steps a day was associated with lower mortality than 4,000 (HR 0.49), as was 12,000 (HR 0.35). Step intensity was not significantly associated after adjustment for total steps (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.65 to 1.27). [abstract] — [Saint-Maurice et al. 2020](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32207799)
- Among 16,741 older women (mean age 72), about 4,400 steps a day was associated with lower mortality than about 2,700. Rates fell progressively before levelling at about 7,500. The authors note that the 10,000 goal has limited scientific basis. Intensity was not clearly related after accounting for total steps. [abstract] — [Lee et al. 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31141585)
- In 78,500 UK adults, more steps were associated with lower risk up to about 10,000 a day, and peak 30-minute cadence was associated with lower risk beyond total steps. This contradicts the null intensity results in Saint-Maurice and Lee. [abstract] — [Del Pozo Cruz et al. 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/36094529)

**Cadence**
- A cadence of at least 100 steps per minute is a consistent heuristic for moderate intensity (3 METs) during sustained, rhythmic walking, despite individual variability. [abstract] — [Tudor-Locke et al. 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29858465)
- In adults aged 21 to 40, thresholds of 100 and 130 steps per minute marked moderate and vigorous intensity. The 41 to 60 age group study found the same. [digest of abstract] — [CADENCE-Adults, 21 to 40](https://doaj.org/article/ed71c96dd4a2492f916805ee4b07c40b)
- In adults aged 61 to 85, thresholds were less clean, ranging from about 102 to 113 steps per minute by method. [digest] — [UMass ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.umass.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/0eb3e886-a799-41eb-9b2d-62680d6edf54/content)

### Inferences
**Counting walks toward activity**
- Show one weekly "active minutes" total against the 150 to 300 range, along with its parts: walks, runs and other.
- Count walk minutes at or above about 100 steps per minute as moderate. Slower minutes can be shown as "easy walking", which still counts as movement but not toward the moderate total. WHO's "any bout counts" means there should be no minimum walk length.
- Use cadence from the walk itself, in rolling one-minute windows, and tolerate stops, so traffic lights do not make a walk fail.
- Count running as vigorous. Under WHO's equivalence, one vigorous minute roughly equals two moderate minutes, so the display can say "120 minutes, counted as 200 moderate-equivalent". For a regular runner this target is likely easy to pass, so the walking number has more meaning as a consistency metric than as a target.
- Muscle-strengthening sessions are counted separately and should not be added to aerobic minutes.
- Because cadence is only a proxy and depends on height and terrain, describe it as "brisk" and not as a measured intensity. Hills can be moderate at a lower cadence. If heart rate data exists, it can upgrade such minutes.

**Steps**
- If the app only records deliberate walks and runs, it does not have all-day steps, and the cohort thresholds do not apply directly because they measured all-day steps. In that case, do not show a steps-per-day target. If phone or watch step data is available, 7,000 to 8,000 a day is a defensible target band. The message should be "more than your usual is good" and not "10,000 or fail".
- Do not imply causation. All of these step studies are observational, and fitter, healthier people walk more.
- Do not show mortality figures in the report. They add anxiety and do not help with next week's plan.

**Metrics for weekly walking**
- Walk count, total minutes, brisk minutes, and share of minutes at brisk cadence compared with own 4-week average. A slow-moving trend such as average cadence on a regular route can be used monthly.
- Walks should be credited as recovery or easy aerobic work on days after hard runs. They should not be framed as a lesser activity than running.

### Gaps
- Evidence on whether intensity adds benefit beyond volume is unresolved: Saint-Maurice, Lee and Paluch point to little or none, while Del Pozo Cruz finds some.
- I did not verify how Ding 2025 handled confounding beyond the abstract and coverage.
- Cadence thresholds are for sustained walking in lab or free-living settings. I found no validation of phone-derived cadence from a tracked walk.
- I found no evidence on how walks should be weighted relative to running in a runner's training load. That choice is left to design.
