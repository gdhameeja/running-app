# Make the plan heavier, the goals kinder

The most valuable changes are not the flagship features of commercial apps. They are four cheap fixes. First, retune the existing bodyweight plan so that it trains heavy relative force and reactive spring, which are the two things that improve running economy. Add foot-core work, which has the best runner-specific injury trial of anything reviewed. Then reframe adherence around a weekly session target with slack and a "welcome back" flow after missed sessions, not daily streaks. Most of this is a few hours to a few days of work in `strength-plan.js` and the home screen. After that come three easy and well-loved gaps from competitors: shoe mileage, GPX/TCX export with Strava upload, and approval-based pace updates in the style of Runna. The goal-race multi-week plan is the largest single gap. It is worth building, but as a rules engine on top of what the app already measures, not as a black box. Its core rules are well supported: mostly easy running with one or two quality sessions, growing the long run only against the longest run of the past 30 days, and a two- to three-week taper that cuts volume 40-60%. What it should *not* copy is equally clear: opaque "AI insight" text, acute:chronic load status labels, daily run streaks and over-aggressive pace progression. Each has evidence or user reports against it. Almost none of the evidence comes from trials of a single recreational runner using an app, so every threshold below is a defensible starting point, not a validated constant.

## Competitors' best ideas are simple rules, and three are missing here

Most of the "smart" features in commercial apps turn out to be small, explainable rules that can run on the phone. Runna's only adaptive mechanism, Pace Insights, gives a pace status after each paced workout. It proposes faster or slower targets only when it sees a *consistent pattern* across sessions, ignores reps that look wrong, never changes paces automatically, and lets the user revert any accepted change ([Runna support](https://support.runna.com/en/articles/14656203-what-are-pace-insights-and-how-do-they-work); [the5krunner](https://the5krunner.com/2026/09/28/is-runna-ai/)). Its July 2026 "Adapt for Heat" slows targets using forecast heat and humidity ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/29/runna-now-automatically-adapts-training-paces-based-on-heat-and-humidity/)). This app already stores weather per run, so the inputs for both exist. Tune-In adapts pace only *within* a run. A Runna-style suggestion after three or more sessions would carry what it learns across runs.

What users love most needs no social graph. **Shoe mileage with a retirement alert** comes up again and again in forum threads, and dedicated apps exist just for it ([Garmin forum](https://forums.garmin.com/apps-software/mobile-apps-web/f/garmin-connect-web/46815/track-shoes); [ShoeCycle](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.shoecycle)). **Strava upload** is easier than it looks. The API takes a generated GPX, TCX or FIT file in one POST and processes it in under two seconds on average. It deduplicates by `external_id` and accepts a JSON format for strength workouts. The rate limits are irrelevant to a single athlete ([Strava API uploads](https://developers.strava.com/docs/uploads/); [Strava rate limits](https://developers.strava.com/docs/rate-limits/)). GPX/TCX export from the stored raw GPS, HR and cadence is the prerequisite, and it also gives the user a standard, tool-neutral backup format alongside the JSON one. Live location sharing is a baseline safety feature: Strava made Beacon free for everyone ([TechRadar](https://techradar.com/news/one-of-stravas-most-important-tools-is-now-free-for-everyone)). It can be built with Supabase realtime and a read-only link, but it is the heaviest of these.

The strongest signal is about what to leave out. Strava's Athlete Intelligence produces generated post-run text, and reviewers judged it "not currently adding much" ([Wareable](https://www.wareable.com/sport/is-strava-premium-worth-it)). Runna's opaque, aggressive progression has been linked by physical therapists to "multiple Runna-related injury cases each week" ([the5krunner](https://the5krunner.com/2026/09/28/is-runna-ai/)). COROS-style 7-day/42-day load ratios are a one-line calculation ([COROS support](https://support.coros.com/hc/en-us/articles/360061452651)). They are still not worth adding as a status label: the largest recent runner cohort found ACWR gave counter-intuitive "protective" results, while single-session spikes predicted injury ([BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751)). The existing weekly report already deliberately excludes such bands. Segments, route builders and heatmaps mostly duplicate the ghost-route feature or need servers the app lacks. In the code, the app has no auto-pause, no lap button and no streak or weekly-target concept. The research only infers that auto-pause and laps delight users and found no source for it, so they rank low.

## Training plans: one load rule, a taper and humble adaptation

For 3-4 runs and 20-40 km a week, the evidence does not pick a winning intensity model, so the app should not either. A 10-week randomised trial in recreational runners found polarized training improved 10K by 5.0% versus 3.6% for between-threshold training. The difference was **not significant** ([PubMed 23752040](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23752040/)). Another trial found a threshold-heavy "focused" 40/50/10 distribution matched polarized 77/3/20 with **17% less training time** ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2019.00070)). Meta-analyses give polarized a small edge on VO2peak only, mainly in programmes shorter than 12 weeks ([PMC11329428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428)), and pool as few as three studies for time-trial benefit ([PubMed 29863593](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29863593/)). The practical default is roughly 75-85% easy running, one quality session a week (two at four runs a week) and a long run. Polarized versus pyramidal can be a user preference, because the choice carries little risk.

The best-evidenced guardrail concerns single sessions, not weeks. In about 5,200 runners followed for 18 months, a session more than 10% longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days raised injury hazard (HR 1.64 for a 10-30% spike, 2.28 above 100%). **Week-to-week change in weekly distance was not associated with injury** ([BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751)). An older novice cohort also failed to support the 10% rule ([JOSPT PDF](https://www.orthobethesda.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/jospt-running-increased-mileage.pdf)). The weekly report already checks this rule after the fact. A plan generator should use the same rule *before* the fact, as the cap on every planned long run. Tapering is just as concrete. A meta-analysis found the best results from a roughly two-week taper with volume cut **41-60%** and intensity and frequency kept ([PubMed 17762369](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/)). Strava data from 158,117 marathoners linked a strict three-week taper to a median 2.6% faster finish ([PMC8506252](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8506252)).

The pace bands should come from VDOT, seeded by a recent race or the app's existing 5K prediction: Easy, Marathon, Threshold at about 88% of vVO2max, Interval at about 3K-5K pace, and Repetition for short reps ([the5krunner VDOT calculator](https://the5krunner.com/tools/running-calculators/vdot-calculator/)). Easy runs should be prescribed by RPE (3-4 of 10) or conversational effort, not pace, so that heat and hills don't turn them into tempo runs. The app's RPE-at-pace drift already checks that easy really is easy. Note that the percentage caps on quality volume (threshold work at most about 10% of the week, intervals about 8%) are Daniels convention reported second-hand, not primary-sourced.

Adaptation should follow the conservative pattern the commercial apps have converged on. TrainAsONE treats "missed sessions as data, not debt": it drops a missed session and adjusts the next one ([TrainAsONE FAQ](https://trainasone.com/faqs/2025/i-missed-a-workout-and-now-it-has-disappeared-how-can-i-do-it-today)). Runna re-plans after several missed runs and offers a "Not feeling 100%" mode lasting up to 14 days ([Runna unwell help](https://intercom.help/runna/en/articles/12809806-feeling-unwell-how-to-adapt-your-training-plan)). Illness guidance is expert opinion only: symptoms above the neck allow easy running, while chest symptoms, aches or fever mean rest. Return with 24 hours fever-free, then about 50% of usual duration ([Runner's World AU](https://runnersworldonline.com.au/running-after-illness/)). The proposed rules are as follows:

* Raise VDOT only after two or more quality sessions are beaten at target RPE.
* Treat a single high-RPE run as a prompt to check heat, sleep or illness, not as a trigger.
* Recompute the 30-day long-run cap after any layoff.
* Offer flexible, repeatable weeks rather than a forced march for beginner or return-to-run blocks. Only 27.3% of a Couch to 5K cohort finished, and dropouts blamed injury and rigid design ([PMC10487403](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10487403/)).
* Offer run-walk as a first-class option. In a randomised marathon trial it was not significantly slower and caused less muscle pain ([BL record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100029893803_0x000001)).

"Norwegian singles" (two or three sub-threshold sessions a week, everything else easy) is popular, plausible and low risk, but it has **no peer-reviewed trial**. The origin story is one self-published author's anecdote ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/244528014-norwegian-singles-method)), and coaches warn that amateurs overestimate their threshold ([O2 Trening](https://o2trening.substack.com/p/the-norwegian-method-for-recreational)). It fits as an optional plan variant for four or more runs a week after a consistent base, with sub-threshold pace set at about T pace plus 5-10 s/km and the RPE drift signal as its brake.

## The strength plan lifts too lightly and skips the two proven extras

The current A/B/C structure is sound. It uses three sets, double progression up variation ladders and two to three sessions a week, which matches a review of 24 studies that found strength work improved running economy by **2-8%** and recommended 2-3 sessions a week ([PMC5889786](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5889786/)). The problem is the type of load. A 2024 meta-analysis of 652 runners found that high-load and combined (heavy plus plyometric) training improved economy. **Submaximal-load and isometric training did not** ([UNAB repository](https://repositorio.unab.cl/items/13e3ac75-9905-4902-81f7-232752e84ae6/full)). Low loads taken to failure match heavy loads for muscle size, but heavy loads win for maximal strength ([PubMed 28834797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)). With no weights, "heavy" has to mean heavy *for the person*: single-leg work, a backpack of books, slow lowering and moderate rep caps. The code works against this in one place. In `strength-plan.js`, reps are capped at `repHigh` only while there is a harder rung above, so on the top rung they climb without limit. A runner who reaches single-leg calf raises off a step (rep range 12-25) or the pistol squat drifts into high-rep endurance work, the "submaximal" category. The fix is to cap the top rung at `repHigh` and then prompt for "add books to the backpack" or "three-second lowering" instead of more reps.

The plan also lacks the two additions with the clearest runner evidence. **Plyometrics** are the second half of the "combined" method. They improve economy at recreational speeds of 12 km/h or below, and the effect grows past 15 sessions, 7 weeks and 2 sessions a week ([UNAB repository](https://repositorio.unab.cl/items/2cf806a8-b611-4964-8dcb-f365dfa88ccc/full)). Expect results only after about 10 weeks ([PMC9653533](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9653533/)). Coaches suggest starting at about 40-120 foot contacts per session, at most twice a week ([Outside](https://www.outsideonline.com/running/training/workouts/ready-for-power-building-plyometrics-take-this-test-and-start-with-these-exercises/?scope=anon); [Pliability](https://pliability.com/stories/best-plyometrics-for-runners)). Those numbers are coach consensus, not trial data. **Foot-core training** cut injuries to **14.0% versus 32.8%** over a year in a single-blind trial in recreational runners ([Physio-network](https://www.physio-network.com/?p=31789)). That is a stronger runner-specific result than any other exercise reviewed, and a self-directed general programme in first-time marathoners showed no effect, 7.1% versus 7.3% ([PubMed 31642726](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31642726/)). The contrast suggests that specificity, dose and adherence matter more than "doing strength". Strength training's injury benefit rises with volume and intensity ([PubMed 30131332](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30131332/)).

Several targeted fixes follow from the evidence:

* **Soleus.** Running modelling finds the soleus highly active at every speed ([PMC3979434](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3979434/)), and straight-knee raises bias the gastrocnemius, so the plan needs a bent-knee calf raise.
* **Copenhagen plank.** The football trial behind it began with a gentler level-one side-lying adduction, used a pain rule for dropping down a level, and gave **41% lower odds** of groin problems ([BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/3/150.abstract)). Once a week maintained the benefit in season, which is what one slot in an A/B/C rotation already delivers.
* **Nordic lowers.** They roughly halved hamstring injuries in team sports ([BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/21/1362)), but a reappraisal called that "inconclusive" ([Physio-network](https://physio-network.com/research-reviews/thigh/why-methods-matter-in-a-meta-analysis-a-reappraisal-showed-inconclusive-injury-preventive-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise)). The exercise is cheap to keep, but the current 6-12 rep range is too many for a Nordic, and the soreness it causes when new argues for keeping it away from key runs.
* **Hip and knee work together.** Combined hip and knee strengthening beats knee work alone for patellofemoral pain ([Nascimento 2018 PDF](https://wikiMSK.org/w/img_auth.php/3/34/Hip_and_Knee_Strengthening_in_Patellofemoral_Syndrome_Metaanalysis_-_Nascimento_2018.pdf)), so direct hip abduction earns a slot.
* **Push volume.** Pressing currently fills four of eighteen slots, two of them bed dips. That is more than a runner needs.

The table below turns this into concrete changes. These are my synthesis: no study has tested this package.

| Session | Current | Proposed change | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | push-ups, squats, backpack rows, glute bridges, bed dips, plank | Open with a 5-8 min plyo block. **Replace bed dips with a bent-knee (soleus) calf raise**: seated with the backpack on the knee, then single-leg bent-knee off a step. Extend the bridge ladder: single-leg bridge, then single-leg hip thrust with shoulders on the bed, then backpack-loaded. Squats: progress rungs at 3x8-12 per leg, with a backpack-loaded Bulgarian split squat rung before shrimp. | Combined method; soleus load; heavy relative load |
| B | pike push-ups, step-ups, towel door rows, hamstring curls to Nordic, calf raises, side plank | Open with the plyo block. Give the Nordic rung its own range of 2-3 sets of 3-6 slow lowers, and progress by range or slowness. Calf raises: cap at 3x15-20 single-leg, then add the backpack and a 3 s lowering. Step-ups: add backpack and higher-step rungs. | Rep caps; Nordic dosing |
| C | bed dips, lunges, towel door rows, back raises, dead bugs, Copenhagen | **Replace dead bugs with a hip-abduction ladder**: side-lying abduction, side plank with top-leg raise, hip hike off a step. (Core is still covered by the Copenhagen plank, plank and side plank.) Add Harøy's side-lying adduction as Copenhagen level 0, with a "pain above 3/10: drop a level" rule. Lunges: add a backpack rung after "off a step". Extend the towel row ladder into table rows: bent-knee, straight-leg, feet raised, then backpack-loaded ([Antranik](https://antranik.org/rows/)). | PFP evidence; Harøy ladder; no-bar pulling |
| Outside A/B/C | none | A **5-minute foot-core finisher** at least 3x a week, attached to easy runs: short-foot holds, toe yoga, single-leg balance, barefoot calf raises. A **monthly single-leg heel-raise test**, aiming for 25 or more per leg against norms of about 21-25 ([Waikato](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/items/c242f3d2-0b0a-4976-abc6-a7f7f9b13874/full)). Use it, plus pain-free hopping, as the gate for single-leg plyos. | Taddei 2020; plyo readiness |

The plyo block should progress in three stages over roughly six or more weeks: double-leg pogos and skips at 40-60 contacts, then travelling and alternating hops at 60-100, then single-leg pogos, bounds and low stair drop-rebounds at 80-120. It needs a new "contacts" unit in the logger. Two scheduling rules belong next to "Up next":

* **Timing.** Do strength after a hard run or on an easy day. Never do it the day before a long run, or do leg-heavy work within 48 hours of a race. Same-day separation of three hours or more largely removes interference ([TrainingPeaks](https://trainingpeaks.com/blog/risks-of-concurrent-training/)).
* **Dose by phase.** Three sessions a week in base, two in build, one or none in taper ([PMC5889786](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5889786/); [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/3/150.abstract)).

An "irritable tendon" toggle can swap the dynamic calf and knee moves for isometric holds such as a wall sit or a mid-range calf hold. These give short-term pain relief ([Physical Therapy First](https://physicaltherapyfirst.com/?p=10327)), but they are no better than dynamic loading over time ([PMC7406028](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7406028/)), so the toggle should be a temporary mode, not the plan. If these changes land, the weekly report's movement-pattern ledger (`PATTERNS`) needs the new ids.

## Adherence comes from weekly goals with slack and rewarded comebacks

The evidence on app features gives an honest but small baseline. Apps and trackers add about **SMD 0.35, or roughly 1,850 steps a day**, and personalisation and messaging are the significant moderators ([BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/2021/01/26/do-smartphone-applications-and-activity-trackers-increase-physical-activity-in-adults/)). Effects shrink at long-term follow-up ([JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2021/4/e26699)), and behavioural goals plus self-monitoring predict success ([PMC6256104](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6256104/)). Almost all of this comes from inactive adults counting steps, so the effect in an already-active runner is probably smaller and untested. The design question is therefore which cheap mechanisms still help in month four.

Four findings point to one design. A field experiment found goals with a small, costly "emergency reserve" (say, two allowed skips) were hit on **up to 40% more days** and helped people re-engage after failing ([Wharton PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf)). In a megastudy of about 61,000 gym members, the best of 54 nudges was a tiny reward for **returning after a missed workout: +27% visits** ([Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/wharton-study-best-ways-boost-workout-habits)). Broken streaks reduce effort, especially when people blame themselves ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)), and run streakers report injury and stress ([medRxiv](https://medrxiv.org/content/medrxiv/early/2024/12/31/2024.12.26.24319676.full.pdf)). Habit research says a single miss doesn't derail automaticity, which takes a median of 66 days to plateau ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)), while consistency at around four sessions a week for six weeks establishes a habit ([PubMed 25851609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)).

The resulting feature has four parts:

* A **weekly session target**, for example four sessions combining runs and strength with one grace skip.
* A **weekly streak with an earned freeze**, never a daily one.
* A **comeback flow** that offers to re-slot a missed session or swap in a minimum version (a 15-minute easy run or a 10-minute circuit), then marks the return as "back on track" with no shame or points-loss copy.
* **Return sessions highlighted** in the Monday report.

The Monday report is also the natural place for an implementation intention. "If-then" planning with barrier management has its own meta-analytic support, g ≈ 0.24-0.31 ([McMaster](https://www.mcmasteroptimalaging.org/full-article/HE/meta-analytic-review-effect-implementation-intentions-physical-activity-25322)), and the start of a week is a documented fresh-start landmark ([Dai et al. 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)). The report could end with "plan this week", where the user picks a day and rough time for each session plus a backup slot. The app would then fire one reminder at that time only if nothing has been logged. Just-in-time prompts lift activity at first, but the effect fades within weeks ([PMC6401341](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6401341)), so reminders must stay sparse and tied to the user's own plan. A consistent time of day is linked to more exercise ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/07/190703121438.htm)). That matters more at home, where there is no gym to act as the cue.

The app's existing self-competition features carry more weight than they appear to. In the STEP UP trial, only the competition arm sustained an effect ([Penn Medicine](https://pennmedicine.org/news/news-releases/2019/september/using-a-wearable-device-to-exercise-more-add-competition-to-improve-your-results)), and ghost races are the closest solo substitute. That is an inference: no trial tests racing your past self. The countervailing risk is quantification. Measuring an activity raised output but **reduced enjoyment**, and people did less once tracking stopped ([Duke Fuqua](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/etkin-counting-steps)). In adults, only autonomy satisfaction predicted enjoyment of tracking ([NTU](https://nanyangtechnologicaluniv.demo.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/self-determination-in-wearable-fitness-technology-the-moderating-)). Two cheap moves follow. Add a "just run" mode that hides pace and voice splits on easy runs but still records everything. Phrase PBs and ghost results as competence ("fitter than eight weeks ago"), not pass/fail. Music has robust acute benefits for mood and perceived exertion ([USQ](https://research.usq.edu.au/item/q57x6/effects-of-music-in-exercise-and-sport-a-meta-analytic-review)). The evidence for narrative running games is only qualitative ([UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10139156/)), so music control is worth having, but story modes are not.

## Conclusion

The research inverts the usual roadmap. The headline gap, a goal-race plan, is real, but the evidence underneath it is thin on what plans should *contain* and firm only on what they should *avoid*: single-session spikes, sloppy tapers, rigid progression and pace targets that update too eagerly. That makes the plan generator mostly a matter of turning the app's existing guardrails into forward planning, and it can wait until the cheaper wins have shipped. The strength side has the opposite profile. Its evidence is specific: heavy relative load, plyometrics and foot-core work. The current plan's main shortfall is that it lets reps climb instead of making each set harder, and fixing that is nearly free.

One idea runs through all four questions: the app should be a strict planner and a forgiving judge. It should hold firm on load rules and exercise difficulty, then be generous about misses, keep goals weekly, and celebrate returns. The biggest unknown is whether any of this works for one already-motivated runner. Nearly all the adherence evidence comes from inactive adults counting steps. The fair test is the app's own data: sessions per week and ladder levels over the next two 8-10 week blocks, compared with the blocks before the changes.
