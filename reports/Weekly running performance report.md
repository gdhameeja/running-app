# Count the week, compare the month

A weekly report for this athlete should describe the week exactly and make claims about fitness only across 4-week blocks, because one week of about three runs cannot resolve a real change: with 5% per-run noise, a week-on-week comparison needs roughly an 8% shift, and realistic short-term gains are 1-3%. The analytical core should be session RPE x minutes for load, one run-length check against the longest run of the past 30 days, an RPE-at-pace residual as the main fatigue signal, multi-day resting heart rate and sleep baselines as context, and separate ledgers for strength (days, hard sets per pattern, ladder level) and walking (minutes, brisk minutes). Leave out readiness scores, ACWR risk bands, the 10% rule, monotony and strain, VO2max, TRIMP as a primary metric, and any status label: the largest runner cohorts contradict the ACWR sweet spot, the one sizeable test of a readiness score found almost no link to same-day running efficiency, and estimated-fitness labels are what users most resent. Sparse wrist heart rate is the weakest input, so it should add a trend when it qualifies and never hold anything else up. The report itself should be a fixed layout delivered at the start of the week: how the week felt, planned versus done, one specific thing that went well, 3-5 numbers against the person's own recent past, one observation, one suggestion. The formula families and many cut-offs are published, but almost none has been validated for one recreational runner on cheap-watch data, and no evidence shows that any weekly summary improves outcomes in any product, so the thresholds here are defensible starting points to tune on the user's own history.

Throughout, **Published** means a finding from a study, **Vendor** means a company or practitioner statement, and **Design** means judgement or arithmetic made for this app with no direct test behind it. Many sources were read only as abstracts or summaries; the last section lists where that matters.

## One week cannot prove a fitness change, so noise sets the rules

**Published.** Noise in most field measures exceeds the smallest worthwhile change, which for individual-sport athletes is about 0.3 of competition-to-competition variation ([Hopkins 2004](https://www.sportsci.org/jour/04/wghtests.pdf)). Race-to-race variation is 1.2-1.9% for fast road runners and up to 2.3 times that for slower ones ([Hopkins and Hewson 2001](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-200109000-00023)), and a treadmill 5 km time trial has about 2.0% typical error ([Laursen 2007](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010f5)). Real gains are small by comparison: recreational runners improved 3000 m time by 1.2-1.7% around a two-week overload block ([Nuuttila 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25020533)). Noise falls as 1/√n, so four tests halve it, and frequent cheap signals beat occasional strong ones ([Buchheit 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3936188/)). Hopkins gives the working rules for one person: estimate typical error from **at least 10 consecutive measurements** around a fitted trend, treat a change above about **2 typical errors** as unusual (accepting 10% false calls each way), and compare with the mean of several earlier tests rather than the previous one ([Hopkins 2017](https://www.sportsci.org/2017/wghtrend.htm)). Regression to the mean makes natural variation look like real change, so a good week after a bad one proves nothing ([Barnett 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15333621/)).

**Design.** The table below applies that arithmetic. It assumes independent observations, which real runs are not, so true uncertainty is larger.

| Comparison, at 5% per-run noise | Standard error | Change needed (2 x SE) |
|---|---|---|
| 3 runs vs 3 runs (week vs week) | 4.1% | about 8% |
| 12 runs vs 12 runs (4-week blocks) | 2.0% | about 4% |
| Slope from 24 runs over 8 weeks | 0.44% per week | ±5.8% (90%) on the 8-week change |
| One time trial vs one (2% noise) | 2.8% | 5-6% |
| Two time trials vs two | 2.0% | about 4% |

Six metrics checked weekly at Hopkins' rule give a 74% chance of at least one false flag every week, and 24% even at a Gaussian ±2 SD. Four rules follow for every metric in the app. First, compare 4-week blocks and an 8-12 week Theil-Sen slope, which tolerates about 29% corrupted points ([Peng et al.](https://math.indianapolis.iu.edu/~hanxpeng/uer.pdf)); expect "no clear change" to be the usual honest answer. Second, use three language tiers from one quantity, SE_diff = 1.25 x σ x √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂) for block medians: below 1.4 SE_diff is "no clear change", 1.4-2 is "probably up or down", and above 2 in two consecutive reports is "clearly". Third, estimate σ from the user's own detrended history (MAD x 1.4826, on log values for speed), give no verdict under 10 observations, and fall back on literature priors until 20, because an SD from 10 points can be 65% too low. Priors are about 3% for exercise HR, about 3 bpm for resting HR ([Buchheit 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3936188/); [Quer 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227709)), and a guessed 3-5% for pace-at-HR measures. Fourth, exclude the current window from its own baseline, exclude paused weeks, rebuild baselines after more than 14 days off or a change of watch, and print n beside every statistic. Display precision should sit above the noise: pace to 5 s/km, sleep to 15 minutes, whole percentages.

## Session RPE and one run-length check replace the load dashboard

**Published: session RPE is the right load measure.** Foster's method multiplies a CR-10 rating, taken about 30 minutes after the session, by duration in minutes ([Foster 2001](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/A%20New%20Approach%20to%20Monitoring%20Exercise%20Training.pdf)). A review of 36 studies reports correlations with TRIMP mostly around r = 0.75-0.86 but as low as 0.50, and reliability ICCs from 0.69 to 0.95 ([Haddad 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/)). Runner validation is thin: in 12 runners the correlation with TRIMP was only r = 0.49, yet session RPE separated session types and TRIMP did not ([Scheltinga 2026](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097300?lng=en)). Heart-rate load has a counter-case in team sport, where TRIMP tracked VO2max change better ([Clemente 2025](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097284)). No study links session RPE load to injury in recreational runners, and no smallest worthwhile change exists for it.

**Published: ACWR fails for runners.** The 0.8-1.3 sweet spot came from elite team sport ([Zouhal 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569/)). In 5,205 runners, uncoupled ACWR above 2.0 was associated with *lower* overuse injury (HRR 0.75, 0.59-0.96) ([Frandsen 2025](https://www.fitasaphysio.com/uploads/4/3/3/4/43345381/1203.full.pdf)). In 435 Dutch runners, ACWR below 0.70 carried 9.6% predicted injury probability against 1.3% above 1.38, and the authors note that low load may be a consequence of developing symptoms rather than a cause ([Nakaoka 2021](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/141958770/The-association-between-the-acutechronic-workload-ratio-and-running-related-injuries-in-dutch-runners.pdf)). A cluster RCT of ACWR-guided load management in elite youth footballers did not reduce health problems, though compliance was 63% ([Dalen-Lorentsen 2021 review](https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/other/does-load-management-using-the-acutechronic-workload-ratio-prevent-health-problems-a-cluster-randomised-trial-of-482-elite-youth-footballers-of-both-sexes/)). Mathematical coupling is the weakest of the criticisms: with a 4-week window the coupled ratio is just 4u/(u+3) of the uncoupled ratio u, a fixed rescale, which fits Coyne's near-identical results ([Coyne 2019](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/7321/)). Arbitrary bands and unjustified causal reading matter more ([Impellizzeri 2020](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Acute:Chronic-Workload-Ratio:-Conceptual-Issues-and-Impellizzeri-Tenan/ede5743a426fd6429d28f8505500a3f771dbcf8b); [Wang 2020](https://read.qxmd.com/read/32125672/analyzing-activity-and-injury-lessons-learned-from-the-acute-chronic-workload-ratio)). Opinion is not unanimous, and one editorial argues ACWR should not be abandoned ([Zouhal 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569/)), but nothing in that case concerns recreational runners.

**Published: weekly percentage rules have no support either.** A graded 10%-rule plan produced 20.8% injuries against 20.3% for a standard plan in 532 novices ([Buist 2008](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/89x92/no-effect-of-a-graded-training-program-on-the-number-of-running-related-injuries-in-novice-runners-a-randomized-controlled-trial)). A review of 36 studies and 23,047 runners graded volume, frequency, intensity and their changes as conflicting and said no universal progression can be issued ([Fredette 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9528699/)). Weekly increases above 30% gave a non-significant HR of 1.59 for distance-related injuries ([Nielsen 2014](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/excessive-progression-in-weekly-running-distance-and-risk-of-runn)), and 20-60% increases raised risk only at day 21 of 98 ([Damsted 2019](https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/the-association-between-changes-in-weekly-running-distance-and-ru/)).

**Published: the single-session signal is the best available.** Garmin-RUNSAFE followed 588,071 sessions and found overuse HRR of **1.64 (1.31-2.05) when a run exceeded 1.1 times the longest run of the prior 30 days**, 1.52 (1.16-2.00) above 1.3, and **2.28 (1.50-3.48) above 2.0**, while week-to-week change showed no association ([Frandsen 2025](https://www.fitasaphysio.com/uploads/4/3/3/4/43345381/1203.full.pdf)). The study is exploratory and observational, with self-reported injuries, distance only and cut-offs chosen in advance rather than derived from data. It has no independent replication, and no trial tests a cap.

**Design.** Make the long-run ratio the only load-related caution, and word it as such. It is a poor predictor in absolute terms: about 0.22 overuse injuries per 100 sessions rises to about 0.36, roughly one extra injury per 700 flagged runs, and 85% of overuse injuries occurred with no spike at all. Its best use is forward-looking: "your longest run lately is 12 km, so 13 km is the top of your usual range". Treat it as two tiers because the dose-response is not clean. Show load as plain numbers against the mean of the prior four weeks, with no colour and no risk language. A weekly bar with the actual number and a personal range is the one vendor pattern worth copying ([the5krunner on Strava](https://the5krunner.com/2025/11/17/strava-relative-effort-guide-tss-2025)), using four weeks rather than Strava's three so a slow ramp is less easily absorbed. The vendor research notes suggest borrowing Coros-style ratio bands; this report rejects that, because those bands are unvalidated vendor numbers and the runner evidence points the other way.

**Published and Design: intensity mix.** No distribution has been shown superior for recreational runners. An individual-participant network meta-analysis found no polarized vs pyramidal difference overall, with a non-significant lean to pyramidal in recreational athletes, and showed that one programme can read 75/8/17 by session goal and 91/6/3 by HR time in zone ([Rosenblat 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02149-3)). One published mapping is RPE up to 4 easy, above 4 and below 7 moderate, 7 or more hard, from a triathlete pilot ([Rohrken 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2020.534688/full)). Report session counts by that mapping, say the method, and set no target. "3 of 4 runs were easy" is supported; "your 80/20 is off" is not. The app's scale should display Foster's anchors, where 3 is moderate, 5 hard and 7 very hard ([Haddad 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/)), because an unanchored 1-10 slider will drift from them. Rest-day and consecutive-day evidence is close to empty, so report those as plain facts against the user's habit ([Fredette 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9528699/)).

## How a run felt beats what a cheap watch measured

**Published: subjective measures are the stronger recovery input.** Across 56 studies, subjective and objective measures generally did not correlate, and subjective measures tracked acute and chronic load with better sensitivity and consistency ([Saw 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4789708/)). In recreational runners under overload, readiness and soreness responded more systematically than resting or exercise HR, though in a small sample ([Nuuttila 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235883/)). On an 11-point scale, one step is a reasonable smallest important change ([Hopkins 2017](https://www.sportsci.org/2017/wghtrend.htm)). Saw's caveats apply: non-training stress moves these ratings, and most of the evidence comes from longer questionnaires, not one post-run tap.

**Design: the RPE residual is the main fatigue signal.** Fit expected RPE against hill- and heat-adjusted speed from the user's own steady runs, then track reported minus expected. It needs no heart rate, survives watch dropouts, and is untested. Combine signals rather than average them: when RPE or feel and resting HR both worsen, say so; when only one moves, call it unconfirmed; when feel worsens and devices look normal, side with feel. Missing ratings are not random, because tired people skip logging, so show the count of unrated sessions.

**Published: wrist HR is weak during running.** A Mi Band class device showed 6-8% mean error against a chest strap, underestimated, and produced extreme readings ([Chow and Yang 2020](https://doi.org/10.2196/14707)). A Mi Band 5 lost accuracy above 100 bpm in cardiac patients ([Kim 2022](https://doi.org/10.5535/arm.22050)), and even a Garmin reached 13% error on variable trail running ([Navalta 2020](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0238569)). Against that, the smallest worthwhile change in exercise HR is about 1%, with about 3% typical error under good conditions ([Buchheit 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3936188/)). The direction is also ambiguous: exercise HR was 1.5% *lower* in a strained state ([Schneider 2020](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0244412)) and long-term overload lowers submaximal HR ([Bosquet 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18308872/)), so a falling HR at the same pace is not automatically good news. The closest peer-reviewed everyday-run metric is an HR-running speed index in 45 recreational runners, whose change correlated with VO2max change at r = 0.49 ([Vesterinen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000000349)); its later use relied on a chest strap, so wrist performance is untested ([Nuuttila 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235883/)). Decoupling and efficiency factor are coaching constructs. TrainingPeaks states there is no hard research rule behind its 5% line and tests decoupling on 1-2 hour steady efforts ([TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/aerobic-endurance-and-decoupling/)). No peer-reviewed reliability for EF, and no validation of any Amazfit watch during outdoor running, turned up.

**Design.** Run HR gets a filter, then a narrow use. Pace at a reference HR from a personal regression is preferred to raw EF, because EF assumes HR is proportional to speed through the origin. At 6% random error, about 36 runs per block would be needed to reach 1% precision, so only large changes are detectable. A trend should appear in the 4-week block only, and "fitter" should never be claimed unless RPE and feel are stable or better. The first job is empirical: measure what fraction of real runs qualify, and whether Health Connect samples come from workout mode or background sampling. If fewer than about one run a week qualifies, drop the metric from the report instead of showing a stale line.

**Published: resting HR is the most trustworthy device signal, and a non-specific one.** Across 92,457 wearers, within-person daily SD averaged 3.03 bpm, weekly range about 3 bpm, seasonal swing about 2 bpm, and individual means spanned 40-109 bpm, so population norms are useless. Quer's unusual-rise definition is more than 2 SD above the previous 20 measurements for at least 3 consecutive measurements within 5 days, at a median of 3 episodes per 2 years ([Quer 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0227709)). Short overload raises resting HR moderately and long overload does not ([Bosquet 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18308872/)), and overreached triathletes showed *lower* resting HR, visible only in weekly averages ([Le Meur 2013](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3182980125)). Rises track infection, stress, alcohol and travel ([Alavi 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01593-2)), yet resting HR linked to illness in only 6 of 20 elite athletes ([Spetz 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12696214/)). Relationships with running performance appear only for averages of at least 3-4 days ([Buchheit 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3936188/)). Each vendor's daily value is opaque, and cheap-watch resting HR has no validation. A resting HR rise should prompt "how do you feel?" and never produce a diagnosis.

**Published: sleep is context, not a score.** Total sleep loss clearly hurts performance, but the effect of 1-3 nights of partial restriction remains unclear, and a universal 7-9 hour target is unlikely to be ideal ([Walsh 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/7/356.abstract)). Morning tasks were largely unaffected in one meta-analysis ([Craven 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y)). Twelve adult cohorts do not support poor sleep as an independent injury risk ([Dobrosielski 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01416-3)), and sleep metrics did not change during overload in recreational athletes ([Nuuttila 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25020533)). Low-cost trackers performed worst of six devices, with errors concentrated on poor nights ([Ong 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2023.11.005)), and motionless wake counts as sleep ([Willoughby 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2024.02.007)). Show duration only, as a multi-night median, and never warn about one night.

**Published: conditions adjustments are modest and borrowed from races.** Performance worsens about 0.2-0.6% per °C WBGT above an optimum near 7.5-15 °C ([Mantzios 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8677617/)), with slower runners affected more ([Ely 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17473775/); [Vihma 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00484-009-0280-x)). None of this is from training runs. HR rose 16 bpm at 35 °C against 3 bpm at 22 °C in cycling ([cardiovascular drift study](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4014508)), and no per-degree running figure exists. Uphill, Minetti's polynomial is Cr(i) = 155.4i⁵ - 30.4i⁴ - 43.3i³ + 46.3i² + 19.5i + 3.6 ([Minetti and Pavei 2018](https://air.unimi.it/bitstream/2434/590382/2/Minetti%20%26%20Pavei%202018%20-%20Update%20and%20extension%20of%20the%20%E2%80%98equivalent%20slope%E2%80%99%20of%20speed-changing%20level%20locomotion%20in%20humans_a%20computational%20model%20for%20shuttle%20running.pdf)), measured over ±45% gradient ([Minetti 2002](https://iris.unibs.it/handle/11379/540545)). Downhill it overcredits: the ratio is 0.60 at -10%, while Strava's real-world model bottoms out at 0.88 near -9% and returns to 1.0 at -18% ([Strava Engineering](https://medium.com/strava-engineering/an-improved-gap-model-8b07ae8886c3)). Wind cost rises with its square ([Pugh 1971](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5574828/)), though published figures disagree ([Davies 1980](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1980.48.4.702)).

**Design.** Use 0.4% per °C WBGT above 12 °C as an equal-effort correction to pace, and do not apply it to HR, because slower pace at equal effort does not cancel a higher HR at equal pace. Exclude warm runs from the HR trend until a personal slope exists. If the local climate puts most runs above the exclusion line, the rule empties the sample, so a personal WBGT slope matters more there and same-season comparison becomes the main defence against seasonal false trends. Do not correct for wind: flag runs above 5 m/s and leave them out of trends, because station wind does not represent a sheltered road. Make no rain or humidity correction beyond WBGT ([Vihma 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00484-009-0280-x)).

**Published and Design: race estimates.** Wearable VO2max has individual limits of agreement of about ±10 ml/kg/min ([Molina-Garcia 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9213394/)). Riegel's formula is well calibrated up to the half marathon but at least 10 minutes too fast for half of marathoners ([Vickers and Vertosick 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5000509/)). A well-paced time trial or parkrun every 6-8 weeks is therefore worth more than any estimate, and it is the only honest route to a race-time range.

## Strength and walking need ledgers, not a shared score

**Published: strength.** WHO recommends muscle strengthening on **2 or more days a week** across major muscle groups and finds no dose-response evidence for higher volumes on health outcomes ([Bull 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/)). Progressive push-up variations improved upper-body strength in a short small trial ([Kotarsky 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29466268)), and push-ups taken to failure matched bench press gains in 18 untrained men ([Kikuchi and Nakazato 2017](https://doaj.org/article/f70cce826f3348b5b4f3ace8cf8ac33e)). Failure is not required for strength ([Grgic 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33497853)). Volume bands of under 5, 5-9 and 10+ weekly sets showed only a trend ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27433992)). Reps-in-reserve estimates are accurate within about 1 rep near failure, and the error exceeds 2 reps at 7-10 left ([Hackett 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27787474)). Quantified load exists only for push-ups, and it is shaky: Ebben's ladder runs from 0.41 to 0.74 of body mass, but the paper's two tables disagree ([Ebben 2011](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/4457/4146)). McGill's preference for short holds over long planks is known only through press coverage ([HuffPost](https://www.huffingtonpost.ca/2018/03/28/how-long-to-hold-a-plank_a_23397921/)).

**Published: strength for running.** Strength training improved running economy 2-8% in distance runners, but the trials used heavy, explosive and plyometric work ([Blagrove 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29249083)). A 2024 meta-analysis found plyometrics effective at speeds up to 12 km/h (small effect) and **no benefit from submaximal or isometric strength work** ([Llanos-Lagos 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01978-y)). That speed band is where most recreational running sits, and plyometrics need no equipment, so jumps and hops are the one bodyweight form that matches the running-economy evidence. On injury, the headline RR of 0.315 covers sports generally ([Lauersen 2014](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24100287)); a short self-directed routine did nothing for 720 first-time marathoners (7.1% vs 7.3%) ([Toresdahl 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31642726/)). "Supports your running" is fair, "cuts injuries by two thirds" is not.

**Design: strength ledger.** Lead with strength days, then hard sets per movement pattern (push, squat or single-leg, hinge, core, and pull). Show the ladder as "Level 4 of 7, 3 x 10". Never headline raw reps: moving up a variation lowers reps while strength rises, so a variation change must display as progress and never as a rep drop. Compare best clean sets only within one variation. Use double progression: when all sets reach 12, move up and restart at 6-8, and suggest a harder variation past 15-20 reps, which also improves effort estimates. Single-leg progressions are the closest no-equipment substitute for heavy load, though no trial supports that. Pull is the known gap without a bar: offer table rows, towel or backpack rows and prone Y-T-W raises, none of which were evidence-checked, and let the user exclude the pattern in settings so the report never nags about it. Credit plain bodyweight sets for general strength and health, not running economy. Foster load applies to strength sessions and gives a common effort scale, but HR load does not: Coros admits its load under-reads strength ([Coros](https://coros.com/stories/coros-metrics/c/training-load)). No study quantifies how much gym work affects run recovery, so keep run load and total load as separate lines.

**Published: walking.** WHO sets 150-300 moderate or 75-150 vigorous minutes a week, and bouts of any duration count ([Bull 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/)). A cadence of at least **100 steps per minute** is a consistent heuristic for moderate intensity in sustained walking ([Tudor-Locke 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29858465)), with 130 for vigorous in adults aged 21-40 ([CADENCE-Adults](https://doaj.org/article/ed71c96dd4a2492f916805ee4b07c40b)) and less clean values past 60 ([UMass](https://scholarworks.umass.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/0eb3e886-a799-41eb-9b2d-62680d6edf54/content)). Step-count benefits level off around 6,000-10,000 a day by age ([Paluch 2022](https://doaj.org/article/c3639c468b4348958202e52793ab979d)), but those are observational all-day counts, and whether intensity adds anything is unresolved ([Saint-Maurice 2020](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32207799); [Del Pozo Cruz 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/36094529)).

**Design: walking ledger.** This app records step cadence only during runs and walks, so it has no all-day steps and **must not show a steps-per-day target**. Count walks, minutes and brisk minutes, using rolling one-minute cadence windows so traffic lights do not spoil a walk. Label them "brisk" rather than "moderate intensity", because phone cadence is unvalidated and hills raise effort at low cadence. Count running as vigorous at 2 moderate-equivalent minutes each, per WHO's equivalence, keep strength out of aerobic minutes, and treat the WHO line as a small footer because a regular runner clears it easily. No evidence says how to weight walks in a runner's load, so keep them out of run load and out of the long-run ratio. Credit them as easy aerobic work, not as a lesser activity.

## The specification: what to compute, and what to leave out

Weeks run Monday to Sunday. "Valid week" means not paused and at least 2 logged days. P marks a published formula or value, P* a published value transferred to a new context, V a vendor or practitioner value, and D a design choice. Unless marked P, treat every threshold as a tunable default.

### Metrics to compute

| Metric | Computation | Threshold or display | Minimum data | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Session load | RPE x moving minutes, for any rated session; prompt about 30 min after; store rated_at | Round to 10 AU; never impute | One rating | P formula; D rest |
| Weekly totals | Sessions, minutes, km by type; run load and total load as separate lines; print "x of y rated" | If under 75% of minutes are rated, show minutes in place of load | 1 session | D |
| Change vs usual | 100 x (this week / mean of prior 4 valid weeks - 1), current week excluded | Within ±15%: "about usual". Above +30%: soft note only. No colours | 3 valid prior weeks at 2+ sessions each; otherwise absolute numbers | P* 30%; D rest |
| Long-run ratio | R = run km / longest run km in prior 30 days; runs only | R > 1.1 "longer than anything lately"; R > 2.0 "large jump". Ignore excess under 0.5 km (D). Show 1.1 x longest as next-run reference | 30 days of history. No run in 30 days: return-to-running note, not a spike | P cut-offs; D wording |
| Days in a row | Longest run of consecutive running days this week | Mention only if above the user's 12-week maximum; no risk language | 12 weeks | D |
| Session mix | Easy RPE ≤ 4, moderate 5-6, hard ≥ 7; counts and minutes, labelled "by session rating"; intervals count as hard | No target. Note if ≥ 75% of runs are moderate for 3 straight weeks with 3+ runs | Rated sessions | P* bands; D rest |
| Adjusted speed | Per smoothed segment: v x f(i), where f = Cr(i)/Cr(0) uphill; downhill f = max(Cr ratio, 0.88), rising back to 1.0 at -18%. Then x (1 + 0.004 x max(0, WBGT - 12)) for pace and RPE work only | Label as equal-effort estimate. Above WBGT 28: no correction, exclude from trends | GPS, smoothed elevation, weather | P grade curve; V downhill; P* heat rate; D cut-offs |
| WBGT | No app-grade formula confirmed. Commonly quoted: 0.567 Ta + 0.393 e + 3.94, with e vapour pressure in hPa. Verify against the BoM source before coding; assumes moderate sun, light wind | Internal only | Temperature, humidity | Unverified |
| Steady-run filter | Continuous, 20+ min after dropping first 10 min, RPE 3-6, CV of adjusted km splits ≤ 8%, wind under 5 m/s | Gatekeeper for the next three rows | Splits, RPE | D |
| RPE residual | Fit RPE on ln(adjusted speed) over steady runs from last 8-12 weeks; residual = reported - fitted; take the median per 2 weeks | Median ≥ +1 for 2 consecutive weeks: note. Single runs never | 10 steady runs to fit; 3+ per 2-week block | P* 1-point step; D rest |
| HR cleaning | Drop samples below RHR + 20 or above max observed + 5; drop jumps over 20 bpm in 10 s with no pace change; flag sustained HR within ±3 of cadence or cadence/2; use median | Coverage = share of 5-min bins with a valid sample. State "no usable HR" plainly; never impute | Cadence for lock check | D |
| Pace at reference HR | Steady, coverage ≥ 60%, WBGT ≤ 20. Fit ln(hill-adjusted speed) = a + b x HR over 12 weeks. Per run: v_ref = v x exp(b x (HR_ref - HR)). HR_ref = median qualifying HR, then fixed. Until 20 runs or if HR spread is under 15 bpm: speed / (HR - RHR) | Median of 4-week block vs prior block, in tiers. Theil-Sen with 8-10+ points over 4+ weeks. "Fitter" needs stable RPE and feel | 6+ qualifying runs per block; 10 for a verdict | P* concept; D formula |
| Decoupling | (EF first half - EF second half) / EF first half | Show only on steady flat runs ≥ 60 min, cool, HR in both halves. Under 5% steady, over 10% marked drift | Rarely met; hide otherwise | V |
| Pace fade | Same formula on adjusted speed, for long runs, beside RPE | Descriptive only | Splits | D |
| Time-trial benchmark | Flagged all-out effort, compared like with like | Single vs single: 5-6% to call a change; 2 vs 2: about 4% | 1 per 6-8 weeks | D, from P noise |
| Race range | t₂ = t₁ x (d₂/d₁)^1.06, up to half marathon, from a benchmark in the last 8 weeks | Show a range of about ±4%, widening with age; no marathon without volume support | One benchmark; never easy runs | P formula; D band |
| RHR unusual rise | Baseline = median of 28 days before last 7; spread = max(2 bpm, MAD x 1.4826). Flag: 3 of last 5 days above baseline + 2 x spread | "Resting HR is up, check how you feel"; list common causes | 14 valid days, 20 preferred | P* Quer; D rest |

### Metrics to avoid

| Avoid | Why | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| ACWR bands, 0.8-1.3 or 1.5 risk labels | Contradicted in runners, null RCT in football, unstable at 2-4 sessions a week | Plain percent vs own 4-week mean |
| 10% weekly rule | RCT null; review calls it unjustified | Long-run ratio |
| Injury risk percentages or scores | No metric predicts injury for an individual; most injuries follow no flag | Neutral caution |
| Monotony and strain | One small 1998 illness study ([Foster 1998](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Monitoring-training-in-athletes-with-reference-to-Foster/59629df5a87418e5653956d54bf8630a102544ae)); reads low in any week with rest days; the 2.0 cut-off is unconfirmed; Foster's worked example does not reproduce | Plain description of the hard/easy pattern |
| TRIMP as main load (Banister, Edwards, Lucia) | Needs continuous HR; gaps silently undercount; Lucia needs lab thresholds; no study of missing data | Optional cross-check at ≥ 80% coverage, never on one trend line with RPE load |
| CTL/ATL/TSB headline | Load history, not fitness; ill-conditioned parameters ([Imbach 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8894528/); [Coggan](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/the-science-of-the-performance-manager/)); TSB bands are in TSS units | 12-week load bars |
| Readiness or recovery 0-100 | No disclosed weights, no outcome validation across 14 scores ([Doherty 2025 summary](https://www.biosourcesoftware.com/post/5-second-science-wearable-composite-health-scores-require-validation)); median r = 0.056 with same-day efficiency, -0.272 with prior-day load, in a non-peer-reviewed test ([Gneta](https://www.gneta.app/blog/garmin-training-readiness-accuracy)) | Raw baselines plus feel |
| Status labels ("Unproductive") | Label flips on one noisy estimate; the main source of user anger ([Slowtwitch](https://forum.slowtwitch.com/t/garmin-consistently-telling-me-im-strained-unproductive/826852)) | Tiers with visible evidence |
| VO2max number | About ±10 ml/kg/min per individual | Time-trial benchmark |
| Single-run EF or weekly fitness verdicts | Below the noise floor; direction ambiguous | 4-week block |
| Sleep stages, sleep score, one-night alerts | Cheap devices worst; sham negative feedback worsened alertness and fatigue ([Gavriloff 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29989248/)) | 7-day median duration |
| 80/20 compliance score | No target established for recreational runners | Session counts by rating |
| Steps per day, mortality figures | No all-day data; observational; anxiety for no action | Walk ledger |
| Push-up norms, firefighter thresholds | Norm sites conflict; Yang's IRR rests on 37 events in one male cohort ([Yang 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30768197)) | Own monthly test |
| Total reps; computed load x reps | Punishes moving up a level; Ebben's tables conflict | Ladder and hard sets |
| Running cadence target | The research notes contain no evidence for any target; cadence is used here for HR checks and walking | Show on the run detail, no judgement |
| Daily streaks | Showing a break cuts later engagement, more with self-blame ([Silverman and Barasch 2023](https://udspace.udel.edu/handle/19716/34160)) | Active weeks in last 8 |
| LLM recap of visible numbers | Reviewers call such text generic and blind to splits and plan ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/wellness/smartwatches/i-tried-garmin-connect-for-a-week-heres-3-things-i-like-and-3-i-dislike)) | Text only when it adds comparison or a next step |

## Six fixed blocks, one observation and one suggestion make the report

**Published.** Feedback improves performance on average (d = 0.41), but over a third of interventions lower it, and effects fade as attention moves from the task to the self ([Kluger and DeNisi 1996](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)). Prompted monitoring promotes goal attainment (d = 0.40), physically recording data beats not recording it, and comparing with one's own past works as well as comparing with a future target (0.43 vs 0.41) ([Harkin 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/)). Self-monitoring paired with goals or feedback beats it alone ([Michie 2009](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/13754)). Tracker effects are mixed ([Ferguson 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35868813); [Finkelstein 2016](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27717766)). False negative sleep feedback worsened how people felt ([Gavriloff 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29989248/)) and, in one study, how they performed ([Draganich and Erdal 2014](https://www.noigroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Draganich_2014_sleep-fatigue-beliefs.pdf)); applying that to training scores is an extrapolation. Counting can reduce enjoyment, and a learning frame removed that in one reading study ([Etkin 2016](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Etkin-Jordan-11-12-15-Hidden-Cost.pdf)). No trial compares weekly with daily feedback, sets an ideal metric count, or shows that any commercial weekly summary improves outcomes.

**Vendor and user reports.** Coaches open with how it felt ([Koop](https://trainright.com/power-of-subjective-feedback-athlete-training/)). WHOOP delivers on Mondays and Oura keeps dismissed reports in an archive ([Oura](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/360046061373-Oura-Reports)). WHOOP's friendlier monthly recap drew complaints for losing depth, trends and shareability ([WHOOP community](https://www.community.whoop.com/t/new-month-in-review-is-a-huge-disappointment/9035)). Garmin offers an illness or injury pause ([Garmin manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals-apac/webhelp/forerunner265series/EN-SG/GUID-6A5D6D66-1F75-45B3-9E37-3AA5C189148F-8221.html)). Forum users act on multi-week trends and traceable anomalies and mute daily verdicts ([TrainerRoad](https://www.trainerroad.com/forum/t/do-you-use-follow-garmin-training-metrics/94984)), a self-selecting sample.

**Design.** Deliver on Sunday evening or Monday morning, so the suggestion lands on a week that has not started and rides the new-week landmark ([Dai 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:60:y:2014:i:10:p:2563-2582)), with one informative notification and no reminders. The layout never changes. Every sentence opens to its inputs, formulas are versioned, and the archive and export keep the long view that WHOOP users missed.

| Block | Content | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Your week | One-tap "easy, about right, hard, too much", shown beside the numbers; then one sentence of counts | Asked before any data is shown |
| 2. Planned vs done | Counts by run, strength, walk | Neutral: "2 runs, usually 3". No "missed" |
| 3. Went well | One specific auto-picked fact: level-up, longest run, best consistency | Skip it rather than inflate |
| 4. Numbers | 3-5 rows, each with this week and 4-week mean: run km and minutes, run load, strength days, walk minutes, active weeks of 8 | 12-week bars behind a tap; neutral colours |
| 5. One observation | Top firing rule below, with its evidence and n | At most one; absent is fine |
| 6. One suggestion | A specific choice for next week, plus the next long-run reference | Choice wording, never "should" |

Every fourth report adds a block review: pace at reference HR or RPE-at-pace, ladder levels, resting HR and sleep trend, time-trial results. A rotating reflection question closes the report ("What got in the way, if anything?", "Any niggle worth watching?"). Stay with the same small set so answers compare over time.

Observation rules run in priority order, and the first to fire wins. Rules 2, 4, 5 and 7 are inferences, so their triggers require persistence or agreement between signals, which guards against the false-flag arithmetic above. Rules 1, 3, 6 and 8 report facts and may fire on a single event.

| # | Trigger | Observation | Suggestion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pain or niggle reported, in the answer or notes | "You mentioned your left calf on Thursday." | Overrides any push for more volume: keep runs easy or short, see someone if it persists |
| 2 | RHR unusual rise plus tired feel | "Resting HR has been up 5 days and runs felt tired. Common causes: illness, poor sleep, alcohol, heat, travel." | Keep next week light, or pause the report |
| 3 | R > 1.1 | "Sunday's 15 km was your longest in 30 days, by 25%. Most runs like this are fine." | Hold the long run near that distance for a week or two |
| 4 | RPE residual ≥ +1 for 2 weeks | "Runs have felt about a point harder than usual at the same adjusted pace, for two weeks." | One easier week, or check sleep and heat |
| 5 | RHR alone, or sleep alone | "Resting HR is 4 bpm above your usual; nothing else has changed. Unconfirmed." | Nothing to change; notice how you feel |
| 6 | Load above +30% | "This week was 40% above your 4-week average." | State it; no instruction unless feel is tired |
| 7 | Nearly all runs moderate for 3 weeks | "Most runs have landed at RPE 5-6 lately." | Try making one run clearly easy |
| 8 | Strength under 2 days, or a pattern absent for 3 weeks | "One strength day this week." | "Try two, for example Tuesday and Friday" |

An illustrative report, with invented numbers, reads like this:

```
WEEK OF 2 NOV          You said: about right

3 runs, 1 strength day, 2 walks. 31 km, 4 h 40 min in total.

Went well: moved up to feet-elevated push-ups, 3 x 8.

               This week   4-week mean
Run km             31          28
Run load          1,120      1,050      3 of 3 rated
Strength days       1           2
Walk minutes       70          85       45 brisk
Active weeks     7 of 8

To notice: Sunday's 15 km was your longest in 30 days, by 25%.
Most runs like this are fine. It was 16 C with light wind.

Next week: keep the long run near 15 km. Try two strength days,
for example Tuesday and Friday.

Any niggle worth watching?                         [why these numbers]
```

Wording stays with behaviour and comparison, and avoids red, "failed", "poor", "should" and "only". A week under 2 logged days becomes a one-line "light week" note with no comparison, and it stays out of baselines. WHOOP's 5-day requirement is a nearby precedent ([WHOOP](https://whoop.com/thelocker/new-weekly-performance-assessment)). Declared illness, travel or rest labels pause comparisons and never count against consistency. New users see counts from week one, load comparison at about week 4, the long-run ratio at day 30, resting HR and sleep baselines at about week 3-5, and block fitness only when enough qualifying runs exist, each with a plain "not enough data yet" state in place of a guess.

## Conclusion

The practical surprise in this evidence is that the sensor-poor setup costs little. HRV, power and precise HR mostly feed composite scores that have not been validated, while the inputs with the best support for one recreational runner are a post-run rating, run distances and a nightly resting HR. The hard work is restraint: an honest report will say "no clear change" most weeks, and its value comes from an accurate record, a credible observation when one exists, and a single next step.

Three uncertainties should shape the build. The Frandsen ratio is an unreplicated observational signal, and the RPE residual, the pace-at-HR filter and every D-marked number are untested, so backtest them on the existing run history and count how often each fires before shipping; a rule firing most weeks is noise and one that never fires is dead weight. Source depth varies: several items came from abstracts or summaries, including Scheltinga, Clemente, Craven, Walsh, Doherty (via a secondary summary) and the WHOOP pages; Gneta is a non-peer-reviewed vendor test; and the WBGT coefficients need checking before use. Finally, no reviewed source tested any weekly report format against outcomes, so the user's own response is the only real test: which observations led to a changed week, and which got ignored.
